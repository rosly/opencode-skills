# Practical requirements lifecycle

Date: 2026-10-05. Scope: a repeatable practitioner workflow from discovery through controlled change, including agile application. The workflow below is explicitly a synthesis, not a lifecycle prescribed verbatim by any one source.

Version anchor: the IEEE Computer Society identifies SWEBOK **v4.0a** as current; its general page retains a “25 September 2025 Update” note, while the currently served PDF identifies itself as “SWEBOK GUIDE V4.0a” and says “Released August 2026” ([official SWEBOK page](https://www.computer.org/education/bodies-of-knowledge/software-engineering); [required SWEBOK PDF](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). The PDF's later dated release is used for the research anchor, and the website discrepancy is retained as a staleness caveat. The current retrievable English IREB Foundation Level syllabus is **3.3.0, 1 April 2026** ([official IREB syllabus PDF](https://hub.ireb.org/media/pages/resources/cpre-foundation-level-syllabus/9c084b1cfd-1787039954/cpre_foundationlevel_syllabus_en_v.3.3.0.pdf)).

## Findings

### Authoritative process coverage

- SWEBOK separates requirements development—elicitation, analysis, specification, and validation—from management—scrubbing, change control, and scope matching. It characterizes the distinction as “reaching an agreement on what software is to be constructed” versus “maintaining that agreement over time” ([SWEBOK v4.0a, ch. 1](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). Its official topic index also explicitly includes “Requirements Prioritization,” “Requirements Tracing,” and “Requirements Change Control” ([IEEE topic index](https://www.computer.org/education/bodies-of-knowledge/software-engineering/topics)).
- IREB groups the major RE tasks as “elicitation, documentation, validation, and management of requirements,” while treating analysis and conflict resolution as part of elicitation ([IREB Foundation Level syllabus 3.3.0](https://hub.ireb.org/media/pages/resources/cpre-foundation-level-syllabus/9c084b1cfd-1787039954/cpre_foundationlevel_syllabus_en_v.3.3.0.pdf)). This differs in taxonomy, not necessarily in needed work: SWEBOK exposes analysis as a separate activity.
- Neither source supports a universal one-pass sequence. SWEBOK says “it is highly unlikely that all requirements work can be done in a single pass,” while IREB says source identification is “an iterative and recursive process that requires constant reconsideration” ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf); [IREB syllabus 3.3.0](https://hub.ireb.org/media/pages/resources/cpre-foundation-level-syllabus/9c084b1cfd-1787039954/cpre_foundationlevel_syllabus_en_v.3.3.0.pdf)).

### Discovery and elicitation

- Begin with context, boundaries, and source coverage rather than immediately collecting feature requests. IREB states: “The quality and completeness of requirements depend greatly on the requirements sources involved” and “Missing a relevant source will lead to an incomplete understanding” ([IREB syllabus 3.3.0](https://hub.ireb.org/media/pages/resources/cpre-foundation-level-syllabus/9c084b1cfd-1787039954/cpre_foundationlevel_syllabus_en_v.3.3.0.pdf)).
- Identify stakeholder classes and non-person sources. SWEBOK defines stakeholders broadly as those involved, affected, or able to influence the outcome, and says “All potential requirements sources should be identified and evaluated” ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)).
- Use a portfolio of techniques selected for the context. IREB distinguishes questioning, collaboration, observation, and artifact-based gathering, plus design/idea-generation, and concludes: “The best results are usually achieved with a combination of different elicitation techniques” ([IREB syllabus 3.3.0](https://hub.ireb.org/media/pages/resources/cpre-foundation-level-syllabus/9c084b1cfd-1787039954/cpre_foundationlevel_syllabus_en_v.3.3.0.pdf)). SWEBOK lists interviews, facilitated workshops, focus groups, surveys, exploratory prototypes, user-story mapping, observation, existing systems, defect data, standards, and regulations ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)).

### Analysis, agreement, and specification

- Elicited statements are candidates, not final requirements. SWEBOK states: “Requirements are unlikely to be elicited in their final form,” and defines analysis as understanding their meaning and implications individually and as a set ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)).
- Analyze each requirement for unambiguity, testability, atomicity, true stakeholder need, stakeholder vocabulary, and acceptability; analyze the set for completeness, concision, internal/external consistency, and feasibility ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). Conflicts should be made explicit and resolved with affected stakeholders rather than unilaterally: “it is unwise for the software engineer to make a unilateral decision” ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)).
- Specification is a communication and memory mechanism, not necessarily one large document: “Requirements specification concerns recording the requirements so they can be both remembered and communicated” ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). Select natural language, structured forms, acceptance criteria, and/or models according to audience, risk, distribution, testing, contractual constraints, and needed traceability.
- Carry enough metadata to manage the item: SWEBOK’s examples include source, rationale, dependencies, conflicts, acceptance criteria, priority, stability, trace tag, and change history ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). Keep implementation locations and test symbols in a separate impact or trace artifact rather than in normative requirement text.

### Validation, prioritization, traceability, and change

- Validate early and repeatedly. IREB’s principle is “Non-validated requirements are useless” and says validation “must start already during RE”; its validation guidance calls for the right stakeholders, multiple views, and “Repeated validation” ([IREB syllabus 3.3.0](https://hub.ireb.org/media/pages/resources/cpre-foundation-level-syllabus/9c084b1cfd-1787039954/cpre_foundationlevel_syllabus_en_v.3.3.0.pdf)). SWEBOK names review, simulation/execution, and prototyping as the three common methods ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)).
- Prioritize to drive delivery and trade-offs. SWEBOK says prioritization “helps focus software engineers on delivering the most valuable functionality soonest” and supports conflict resolution and scope matching; it cautions against “overly rigorous measurement scales or debating small differences” ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)).
- Trace with an explicit purpose. SWEBOK gives two: checking consistency/coverage across work products and impact analysis. It describes tracing backward to source material and forward to design, code, tests, and user documentation ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). This supports selective, risk-based tracing rather than maximal linking by default (inference).
- Control change through a visible decision and closure path. For plan-based work, SWEBOK specifies a request mechanism, optional impact analysis, an accountable decision-maker, stakeholder notification, and tracking accepted changes to closure ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). It also says documented requirements “should be subject to the same configuration management practices as the other deliverables” ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)).

### Empirical agile evidence and limits

- A systematic review covering literature through June 2013 identified “17 practices of agile requirements engineering,” five traditional RE challenges reported overcome, and eight challenges introduced by agile RE; it also cautioned that “more empirical results are required,” especially for non-functional requirements and self-organizing teams ([Inayat et al., required DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.10.046); [University of Twente record and abstract](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/bab052c3-1e72-40ba-a9c7-5d570d9ba3d5)). This is historical evidence, not a current normative standard.
- A 2017 survey of representatives from 92 organizations found agile RE concentrated on free-text requirements elicited through varied techniques; “Often, traces between requirements and code are explicitly managed” and testing and RE were aligned. It also identified unclear requirements and communication flaws as important problems ([Wagner et al., required PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.08360)). The paper reports interviews, prototypes, and facilitated meetings as the most frequent elicitation techniques and says acceptance criteria were common.
- The same survey warns against equating agility with no requirements discipline: “agile RE is not so different from classical RE after all” in elicitation, documentation, change, and improvement ([Wagner et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.08360)).
- A seven-company large-scale systems study found “24 challenges that we group into six areas,” based on 20 interviews, five focus groups, and eight cross-company workshops ([Kasauli et al., required ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0164121220302417); [retrieved full text](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/522021/file/522021_Fulltext.pdf)). Its authors explicitly state that proposed solutions need more validation: “Further validation of the collected solutions is needed.”
- At scale, local team ordering is insufficient where dependencies span teams: the study reports “Bottom-up prioritization is not working well” and finds no proven strategy that fits every context ([Kasauli et al. full text](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/522021/file/522021_Fulltext.pdf)). It also reports that incremental requirements complicate V&V planning and that incomplete traceability can leave tests aligned with outdated requirements.

## Sourced practical workflow

**Status: the sequence, gates, and records below are an inference synthesized from the sources above.** It is intended to be repeatable but tailorable; it is not quoted as a standard-mandated lifecycle.

1. **Frame the decision and context.** Record the business/user problem, desired outcomes, scope boundary, external interfaces, constraints, assumptions, regulatory obligations, and the decision owner. Define what “good enough to proceed” means for this initiative. This operationalizes IREB’s context and source emphasis and SWEBOK’s distinction between true need and proposed solution.
2. **Map requirement sources and stakeholders.** Create a source/stakeholder map including underrepresented and negatively affected groups, operations/support, regulators, existing systems, policies, defect/usage evidence, and standards. Assign an owner and revisit trigger. Gate: relevant source classes have either participated or have a recorded reason for absence.
3. **Elicit candidate needs with mixed techniques.** Select at least complementary techniques appropriate to uncertainty and access—for example interview plus observation/artifact analysis, or workshop plus prototype. Capture origin, rationale, assumptions, and unresolved questions with each candidate. Do not treat elicitation output as approved scope.
4. **Analyze and negotiate.** Separate problem/need from solution ideas; test atomicity, clarity, measurability/testability, feasibility, consistency, dependencies, quality attributes, and constraints. Expose conflicts and negotiate with affected decision-makers. Gate: record unresolved conflict and uncertainty in design or decision material rather than silently converting it into active requirements.
5. **Specify for the consumers and risk.** Choose the lightest representation that reliably communicates: story/use case or structured statement, models where relationships or behavior require them, measurable quality constraints, and acceptance examples/criteria. Assign hierarchical identifiers that identify both the owning module and requirement subgroup, plus source, rationale, owner, priority, dependencies, acceptance criteria, status, version, and change history when those attributes serve a known management need. Allocate a new identifier when a requirement moves to another module or subgroup.
6. **Validate before commitment and throughout refinement.** Use stakeholder review plus an executable or experiential technique where risk warrants it: prototype, simulation, example, acceptance test, or model execution. Validate need coverage, agreement, clarity, consistency, completeness for the current horizon, feasibility, assumptions, and standard compliance. Record findings and corrections separately. Gate: relevant stakeholders agree the item is sufficiently understood for its next commitment horizon.
7. **Prioritize and match scope to capacity.** Rank by value/need, cost, risk, urgency, dependencies, and cost of delay as appropriate; avoid false precision. Reconcile business and technical views, and at scale coordinate across affected teams. Commit only capacity-feasible scope; explicitly defer or reject the rest with rationale.
8. **Establish purposeful traceability.** Decide which questions traces must answer—for example source legitimacy, design/code/test coverage, regulatory evidence, dependency coordination, or change impact. Maintain only the links needed for those questions, and verify critical bidirectional chains. Gate: high-risk requirements have demonstrable coverage to acceptance evidence and affected implementation elements.
9. **Baseline/commit and control change.** Put durable requirements and linked artifacts under version/configuration control. Route proposed changes through request/backlog entry, impact analysis proportionate to risk, accountable accept/reject/defer decision, notification, reprioritization, implementation, revalidation, and closure. Preserve superseded normative text in version control, remove it from the active requirement set, and retain its disposition, successor, and reason in the lifecycle map.
10. **Monitor outcomes and improve the process.** After release and at regular retrospectives, compare delivered behavior and user/business outcomes with the requirement and acceptance evidence; inspect escaped misunderstandings, churn, trace gaps, and stakeholder latency. Adapt elicitation, representations, validation, and trace depth. This closes the loop rather than treating acceptance as the end of requirements work.

For repeatability, a team can use one compact state flow—**candidate → analyzed → specified → validated → prioritized/committed → implemented/verified → accepted or changed**—while permitting backward movement whenever evidence changes (inference). Each transition should have an accountable owner, observable entry/exit evidence, and retained rationale (inference).

## Agile considerations

- Agile changes the cadence and commitment horizon, not the need for elicitation, analysis, specification, validation, prioritization, traceability, or management. SWEBOK says that under agile, requirements “change, grow, or be eliminated continuously,” and IREB describes iterative RE as adding or modifying requirements “in every iteration” ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf); [IREB syllabus 3.3.0](https://hub.ireb.org/media/pages/resources/cpre-foundation-level-syllabus/9c084b1cfd-1787039954/cpre_foundationlevel_syllabus_en_v.3.3.0.pdf)).
- Run the workflow continuously at multiple horizons (inference): discover outcomes and major constraints at product level; refine near-term backlog items just in time; validate during refinement and delivery; accept against explicit criteria; feed production/user evidence back into discovery.
- Treat a change request as a backlog item but retain decision rigor. SWEBOK says it becomes “just another item on the product backlog” and is accepted when prioritized into an iteration ([SWEBOK v4.0a](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)). Inference: for safety, regulation, contracts, shared interfaces, or large cross-team impact, backlog ordering alone is not enough—retain explicit impact analysis, authorization, notification, and baselines.
- Make acceptance criteria and tests part of the requirement conversation. The 92-organization survey found it “common to define acceptance criteria” and that testers participated in requirements reviews in about half the surveyed projects ([Wagner et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.08360)). Inference: involve testers and operational stakeholders during refinement, not only after implementation.
- Preserve “just enough” documentation according to risk and longevity, not ideology. Large-scale evidence says system-level knowledge, rationale, traceability, backward compatibility, and V&V planning remain difficult when information is distributed across teams ([Kasauli et al.](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/522021/file/522021_Fulltext.pdf)).
- Coordinate priority and completeness across levels. A product owner/backlog can serve one team, but large-scale dependencies require cross-team business/technical forums, an explicit system view, and agreed completeness horizons (inference from Kasauli et al.; the study does not validate one universal framework).
- Keep feedback loops short and inspect RE itself. The survey found continuous RE improvement widespread and motivated mostly by perceived efficiency and identifying strengths/weaknesses ([Wagner et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.08360)).

## Open questions

- What regulatory, contractual, safety, security, or audit obligations determine mandatory artifacts, approvals, baselines, and trace depth in the target organization?
- Which outcome/value model should govern prioritization, and who resolves conflicts between business value, technical risk, compliance, and cross-team dependency?
- What is the required completeness horizon: next story, sprint, release, contract milestone, system integration, or whole product lifecycle?
- Which traceability questions are worth their maintenance cost, and which links can be generated reliably from repositories, tests, and delivery tooling?
- How should system-level requirements and V&V remain synchronized with team backlogs in large-scale or hardware/software development? Kasauli et al. identify this as incompletely solved, not as a settled best practice.
